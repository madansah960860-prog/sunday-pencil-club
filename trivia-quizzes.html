Trivia Quizzes

By the Sunday Pencil Club Editorial Team · Last reviewed: October 2026

How the quizzes work

Each round above gives you a question and four answers; click the one you think is right and the quiz tells you at once, turning the correct choice green. Your score keeps itself at the bottom of each round, though the only person you are playing against is yourself.

There is no time limit and no penalty for a wrong guess — a wrong answer simply shows you the right one, which is how most of us learned anything worth knowing. Work through a round in one sitting or dip in and out; the questions will wait.

A word about our categories

We keep our trivia in five friendly stacks, each chosen because it rewards a lifetime of ordinary curiosity rather than specialist study.

U.S. Geography roams the states, capitals, rivers, and landmarks of this large and varied country — the kind of facts that settle an argument at the dinner table. Americana gathers the flags, holidays, and symbols we all grew up with. Nature wanders the garden and the wider world of animals, trees, and seasons. History & Invention revisits the people and firsts that shaped the modern world, from the light bulb to the Moon landing. And Everyday Knowledge is the homely arithmetic of life — how many inches in a foot, how many cards in a deck.

Checked, and open to correction

Every answer here is a long-settled fact, and we check each one against reliable references before it joins the bank. Still, we are human, and a typo or a slip can occasionally sneak past the proofreader. If you ever believe an answer is wrong, please tell us — a short note to our editors is all it takes, and we will look into it and fix it if you are right. We would far rather be corrected than be mistaken.

Tip: Trivia is best enjoyed as a shared pleasure. Read a round aloud at the table and let everyone call out their guesses; a quiz is twice the fun with company.

The particular pleasure of trivia

There is a special delight in knowing a thing for no reason at all. The capital of a state you will never visit, the name of the first man on the Moon, the number of cards in a deck — these are facts that serve no practical purpose and yet bring a small, warm glow when they surface at the right moment. Trivia is the celebration of exactly that kind of knowing: useless, charming, and quietly satisfying.

It is also a wonderful keeper of company for the memory. Each question is an invitation to reach back into everything you have read, watched, and lived through, and to feel the pleasant click of a fact arriving. When it does not arrive, the answer is handed to you gently, and more often than not you find you half-knew it after all. There is no losing at trivia, only remembering and learning, and both are good for the spirit. Best of all, the facts you gather here are the companionable kind — the sort you can carry to a dinner table or a telephone call and share with someone you love.

The quiet social value of a good fact

There is a reason trivia has always thrived in company — at the pub, around the holiday table, on the long drive. A well-placed fact is a small gift to a conversation. It gives people something to agree about, argue over, and laugh at together, and it draws the quieter members of a gathering into the talk, since everyone knows something that surprises the rest. A shared quiz is really a shared excuse to enjoy one another's company.

For families spread across the generations, trivia is especially kind. The grandparent who knows the old geography and the grandchild who knows the new inventions each hold a piece of the board, and both get to feel clever and useful in turn. We have heard from readers who make our rounds a fixture of their family visits, and nothing could please us more. A fact kept to yourself is a small pleasure; a fact shared is a far larger one.

Getting better, if you care to

You need do nothing at all to enjoy these quizzes — but if you find you want to sharpen up, the method is pleasant and simple. Read widely and without pressure: a good newspaper, a library book on a subject that catches your eye, a documentary on a wet afternoon. Trivia is simply the residue of a curious life, and the more corners of the world you wander into, the more of it you gather without ever trying.

When a question catches you out, pause a moment over the answer rather than rushing on. The facts that stick are usually the ones that surprised us, and giving a surprising answer a few seconds of real attention is enough to lodge it for good. Keep a notebook if you like, and jot down the ones that got away; the simple act of writing a fact in your own hand is one of the oldest memory tricks there is.

Trivia is better with company

A quiz is a fine solitary pleasure, but it comes alive at the kitchen table. Read the questions aloud and let everyone call out a guess before you reveal the answer; the arguments and the laughter are half the point. It is a wonderful thing to do with grandchildren, who are often startlingly good at the newer facts and delighted to stump a grown-up, and it gives every generation a chance to shine on its own home ground.

A closer look at the five rounds

Each of our categories rewards a slightly different corner of a well-lived life, and it is worth knowing what to expect from each. U.S. Geography roams the physical country — the largest state, the longest river, the capital you can never quite recall — the sort of facts that settle a friendly argument and quietly impress the grandchildren. It is knowledge gathered from a lifetime of maps, road trips, and weather reports, and most of us know far more of it than we think.

Americana gathers the shared furniture of the nation: the stripes on the flag, the date of a holiday, the face on a coin, the bird on the seal. These are the facts we absorbed as children and carry without effort, and there is a cozy pleasure in finding them still firmly in place. Nature wanders the garden, the woods, and the wider animal world — the tallest tree, the busy honeybee, the robin that heralds spring — rewarding anyone who has ever looked closely at the living world around them.

History & Invention revisits the people and firsts that built the modern age, from the light bulb to the telephone to the first footprints on the Moon. And Everyday Knowledge celebrates the homely arithmetic of ordinary life: inches in a foot, days in September, cards in a deck. It is the most democratic category of all, for it asks only what a practical person picks up simply by living.

Where a good question is born

People sometimes imagine trivia questions are pulled from some vast machine, but ours begin the old-fashioned way: with curiosity. An editor reads something interesting — the height of a famous tree, the year of a famous first — and thinks, “that would make a nice question.” The fact is then checked against a reliable reference, dressed in a clear sentence, given three plausible wrong answers to sit beside the right one, and added to the bank. It is slow, pleasant, human work, and we would not have it any other way.

The wrong answers deserve a word of their own, for they are quietly important. A good question offers wrong choices that are tempting but fair — near neighbors of the truth, not wild impossibilities. The aim is to make you think for a moment, to feel the small tug of a plausible decoy, and then the satisfaction of choosing well. A question with silly wrong answers is no challenge at all, so we take as much care over the decoys as over the truth.

On being wrong, gracefully

Here is a small philosophy that makes trivia far more enjoyable: treat every wrong answer as a gift. When you guess wrong and the quiz shows you the truth, you have not failed — you have just learned something, and learned it in the most memorable way, with a little jolt of surprise to help it stick. The people who enjoy trivia most are not the ones who know everything, but the ones who are cheerfully unbothered by not knowing.

So guess freely, and never mind the misses. A quiz with no wrong answers would teach you nothing and bore you quickly; it is the near-misses and the happy surprises that make it worth returning to. Keep that spirit and you will find these rounds a pleasure whether you score two or ten. If you enjoy them, the Daily Quiz serves up ten new questions drawn from the whole bank, changing automatically with the date. And for a different sort of challenge, the Brain Teasers ask you to reason rather than recall.
