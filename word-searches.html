Themed Word Searches

By the Sunday Pencil Club Editorial Team · Last reviewed: October 2026

American Places

Our first grid hides the names of American cities and states. Some run left to right, some top to bottom, and a few slant along the diagonal — so keep your eyes moving in every direction.

In the Garden

The second grid is planted with flowers, trees, and the green things that make a garden. A gentle one, this, and a pleasant way to pass a rainy afternoon.

In the Kitchen

Our third grid is full of the tools of the kitchen — the spoons, kettles, and whisks of a well-loved cook's drawer.

How to play, and a few finding tricks

Playing is simple: click the first letter of a word you have spotted, then click its last letter. If the straight line between them spells one of the words in the list, it lights up and crosses off automatically. Words can read forwards or backwards, so do not be shy about trying both ends.

  1. Hunt one letter at a time. Pick an uncommon first letter from your word list — a K, a W, a Z — and scan the grid for just that letter. There are far fewer of them to check.
  2. Scan in lines, not patches. Run your eye straight along each row, then each column, then the diagonals. A steady sweep beats a frantic dart.
  3. Mind the diagonals. These are the words most people miss. Once you have cleared the rows and columns, tilt your head and look corner to corner.
  4. Use Reveal when you are done. If one stubborn word hides to the end, the Reveal button shows them all, so a single holdout never spoils the fun.

Tip: A word search is one of the few puzzles you truly cannot lose — every word is in there, waiting. That makes it the perfect thing for a tired evening when you want a win without a fight.

Taking them with you

Each grid prints cleanly with the Print button, word list and all, in plain black on white. A printed word search is a fine companion for a journey, a waiting room, or a porch on a warm evening, and it asks nothing of batteries or signal. Print a few before a trip and you will never be stuck for something gentle to do.

A gentle exercise in paying attention

What a word search really trains is attention — the simple, underrated skill of looking carefully at one thing until it gives up its secret. In a world that constantly pulls the eye in a dozen directions at once, there is something quietly restorative about a task that asks you to do the opposite: to settle, to focus, to let the rest of the noise fall away while you scan a single quiet grid. Many readers find it as calming as a cup of tea, and for much the same reason.

It is a forgiving sort of focus, too. Unlike a crossword, which can stall you completely, a word search always offers somewhere to look next; there is never a dead end, only a word not yet found. That makes it the ideal puzzle for a wandering mind or a tired evening, when you want to be gently occupied rather than sharply tested. Keep one by your chair for exactly those hours when you would like company for your attention but not a challenge to your wits.

The quiet history of the word search

The word search is a comparative newcomer among puzzles. It is generally traced to Norman Gibat, who published one in a small Oklahoma newspaper in 1968. Teachers took to it at once as a painless way to help children learn spelling lists, and from the classroom it spread to puzzle books, newspapers, and waiting rooms the world over. Within a few years it had become one of the most popular pastimes in print, and it has never really gone out of fashion.

Part of its charm is how little it demands. A crossword asks you to know things; a word search asks only that you look. The answers are all there in front of you, hidden in plain sight, which makes it the most forgiving of puzzles and the most reliably soothing. There is no way to be wrong, only words not yet found, and every grid ends in a small, certain victory.

Why a word search suits everyone

Because it asks for attention rather than knowledge, the word search welcomes solvers of every age and background equally. A child learning to read and a grandparent enjoying a quiet afternoon can share the very same grid and both come away pleased. There is no cultural knowledge required, no vocabulary to have memorized, no trick to have learned — only the simple, steady act of scanning for a shape your eye already knows.

That gentleness is exactly why we include it. On a tired evening, when a crossword feels like too much and the mind wants rest more than challenge, a word search offers a pastime that is calming without being empty. It keeps the eyes and attention lightly engaged, delivers a small reward with every word found, and never once makes you feel that you have failed. For many of our readers it has become the puzzle they reach for when they want to unwind rather than strive.

Making your own, for a rainy day

Word searches are among the easiest puzzles to make yourself, and crafting one for a grandchild or a friend is a lovely afternoon's project. Choose a theme — their favorite animals, the names of the family, the towns you have lived in — and write out eight or ten words. Draw a grid on graph paper, write each word in along a row, a column, or a diagonal, letting them cross where letters match, then fill every empty square with a random letter. Pass it along with a smile, and you have given a gift that no shop could sell.

A word about our three themes

We chose our three themes to be warm and familiar, the sort of words that come easily to mind and feel pleasant to find. American Places gathers the names of cities and states from across the country — Boston and Denver, Oregon and Maine — a gentle tour of the map without leaving your chair. For many solvers, each name carries a memory: a place lived in, visited, or long dreamed of, and that small flicker of recognition is part of the pleasure.

In the Garden is planted with the flowers and trees that fill a well-loved yard: the rose and the tulip, the maple and the fern, the lilac and the daisy. It is a restful grid for a gardener, or for anyone who likes to picture a border in bloom while the rain comes down outside. In the Kitchen rounds out the set with the honest tools of the cook's drawer — the kettle, the whisk, the ladle, the teapot — the familiar companions of a lifetime of meals made with care.

We keep the word lists to common, everyday words on purpose. A word search should be a comfort, not a vocabulary test, and there is a special satisfaction in finding a word you know as well as your own name hidden cleverly among the letters.

A puzzle for every mood

One of the quiet virtues of the word search is how well it fits whatever frame of mind you bring to it. On a sharp, clear morning you can race through a grid at speed, hunting words as fast as your eye can fly. On a tired, foggy evening you can drift through the very same grid slowly, finding one word, setting it down, and coming back later, with no penalty for the pause. The puzzle bends to your mood rather than demanding you rise to its.

That flexibility makes it a reliable friend for the harder days as well as the easy ones. When a crossword feels like too much and the mind wants comfort more than challenge, a word search asks almost nothing and still rewards you with that small, steady drumbeat of little victories — found, found, found. Keep one within reach for exactly those moments. There is no shame in choosing the gentlest puzzle on the shelf; some days, gentle is precisely what is called for.

A pastime to share

Like so many of the gentlest pleasures, a word search doubles in value when it is shared. Set one between two people and race, good-naturedly, to find the next word. Hand one to a grandchild and search side by side, letting them feel the small triumph of spotting one before you do. Because the puzzle asks only for sharp eyes and not for any special knowledge, it levels the ground between the generations in the nicest possible way.

It travels beautifully, too. A printed grid needs no screen, no signal, and no charger, which makes it the ideal companion for a long car journey, a train ride, a waiting room, or a quiet hour on the porch. Print a few before you set out and you will always have something calm and absorbing to hand, for yourself or for a restless young traveler in the back seat.
