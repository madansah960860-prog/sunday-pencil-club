How to solve a crossword, step by step

If it has been a while since you last filled in a grid — or if you never quite got the knack — a few friendly habits turn a crossword from a wall into a pleasure. None of this is cleverness; it is simply knowing where to push.

  1. Read every clue once before you write anything. Your mind will quietly solve two or three of them in the background while you read the rest, and you will come back to an easier grid than you started with.
  2. Start with the answers you are sure of. Fill in the gimmes first — the short words, the obvious ones. Every letter you place gives you a free clue in the crossing direction.
  3. Use the crossings. A down answer you cannot see often becomes obvious once an across answer has dropped a letter or two into it. The grid helps you; let it.
  4. Mind the little words. Three-letter answers (like TEA in today's first puzzle) are the mortar between the bigger bricks. They fall quickly and open up everything around them.
  5. If you are stuck, walk away. A clue that looks impossible now will often be obvious after a cup of tea. The answer was there all along; your mind just needed a breath.

Tip: Count the squares. The number of white cells tells you the length of the answer, and the length alone rules out most of your wrong guesses before you write them.

Reading a clue the way the setter meant it

Most clues in a friendly crossword are definitions, plain and simple. “A hot drink brewed from leaves” wants TEA; “where flowers and vegetables are grown” wants GARDENS. The trick is to match the length of the answer to the sense of the clue. If the clue could mean several things, the crossing letters will choose for you.

Watch the small signals, too. A clue written in the plural is asking for a plural answer, usually ending in S. A clue phrased in the past tense wants a past-tense answer. These tiny hints quietly narrow the field, and noticing them is most of the skill.

Our puzzles keep the clues honest and kind. There are no fiendish tricks, no obscure abbreviations, and nothing you would need a specialist's dictionary to confirm. If you know the word, you will recognize the clue; and if you do not, the crossings will coax it out of you.

Solving on the screen — or on paper

You can solve these grids two ways, whichever suits you.

  1. Click any white square and type a letter; the cursor moves along on its own, so you can type naturally.
  2. Press Check at any time to see which squares are right — correct letters turn a cheerful red.
  3. Stuck for good? Press Reveal answers to fill the whole grid, then try the next puzzle.
  4. Prefer pencil and paper? Press Print and the grid comes out clean, ready for your favorite chair.

Use the three puzzle names above the grid to switch between today's themes. Each is a fresh seven-by-seven, built by hand with everyday words. Start with “Everyday,” warm up on “Around the House,” and finish in “The Great Outdoors.”

A little history of the crossword

The crossword is younger than you might think. The first recognizable one appeared in a New York newspaper in December 1913, the work of an editor named Arthur Wynne, who called it a “word-cross.” It was an immediate hit, and within a decade the puzzle had spread to papers across the country and across the ocean. For more than a century now, the daily grid has been a small ritual for millions — over breakfast, on the train, in the quiet of an evening.

What has kept it alive so long is its perfect fairness. Every answer can be checked against its crossings, so the puzzle never lies to you; if your letters agree in both directions, you are right. That honesty is why a crossword rewards patience so reliably, and why a finished grid feels so satisfying. You did not guess your way to the end — you reasoned your way there, one honest square at a time.

Our grids are smaller than the grand Sunday puzzles of the big papers, and that is on purpose. A seven-by-seven is the right size for a coffee break: long enough to be a real challenge, short enough to finish before the cup goes cold.

The friendly kinds of clue

Big-city crosswords sometimes lean on puns, anagrams, and hidden tricks that can leave a casual solver baffled. We keep to the kinder traditions, and it helps to know the few honest shapes a clue can take.

The plain definition is the backbone of every grid here: “a hot drink brewed from leaves” for TEA. The clue simply describes the answer, and your job is to find the word that fits both the meaning and the number of squares. The example clue points at a category: “a gull, for example” wants a kind of bird, and the crossings tell you which one. The fill-in-the-blank gives you part of a familiar phrase and asks for the rest, which is often the easiest sort of all.

What you will never meet in our puzzles is a clue that tries to deceive you. If you know the word, the clue will lead you to it; if you do not, the letters crossing in from other answers will hand it over. That fairness is a promise, and it is what makes a gentle crossword such good company on a quiet morning.

Making it a gentle habit

There is a reason so many people keep a daily date with the crossword. It is a complete, winnable thing in a world full of unfinished business — a small puzzle you can start and finish in one sitting, with a clear, satisfying end. Unlike so much that fills a day, it asks nothing of you but your attention and gives back the quiet pleasure of a grid filled in.

If you would like to make it a habit, keep it small and keep it pleasant. One puzzle with your morning coffee is plenty. Do not keep score, and do not scold yourself for reaching for Reveal — even lifelong solvers get stuck, and a revealed answer is simply a word you will recognize more quickly next time. The goal is enjoyment, not achievement. Start with the “Everyday” grid, and when you have the measure of it, the other two will feel like old friends.

Fair play, and a word on difficulty

We build our crosswords to one firm rule: every puzzle must be solvable by a reasonable person having a reasonable day. That means ordinary words you already know, clues that describe rather than deceive, and enough crossings that no single tricky answer can lock you out of a corner. If you find one of our grids genuinely unfair, that is a fault on our side, not yours, and we would like to hear about it so we can mend it.

Difficulty, of course, is partly in the eye of the solver. A clue that one person finds obvious will stump another entirely, depending on what each has read and lived. That is the nature of the game and part of its charm; it is why solving with a friend works so well, and why a grid that beats you today may fall easily next week. Do not take a hard puzzle as a verdict on your wits. Even the most seasoned solvers keep a few clues unsolved and come back to them later, and the pleasure is in the return as much as the finish.

Our three grids rise gently in challenge, from the plain words of “Everyday” through the household terms of “Around the House” to the slightly wider vocabulary of “The Great Outdoors.” Start wherever you like, but if you are rusty, the first is the kindest place to warm up.

Solving with a friend

A crossword is usually pictured as a solitary pleasure, but it makes wonderful company for two. Sit side by side and read the clues aloud, each tossing in guesses until an answer clicks. One person often sees what the other cannot, and the back-and-forth turns a quiet puzzle into a warm conversation. It is a gentle, companionable thing to do over a shared pot of tea, and a lovely way to pass time with a spouse, a friend, or a visiting grandchild.

Solving together has a practical benefit, too. Between two memories, many more clues fall, which keeps the puzzle moving and the mood cheerful. And when both of you are stumped, the Reveal button is there without the slightest shame — a shared “of course!” at the answer is its own small reward. However you solve, remember there is no clock and no judge here; the only aim is the pleasant hour itself.

A seven-by-seven is the right size for a coffee break. When you have conquered all three, the word searches make a relaxing next stop, and the brain teasers wait for an evening when you want a longer think.
