Brain Teasers

By the Sunday Pencil Club Editorial Team · Last reviewed: October 2026

Riddles, logic puzzles, and a number teaser or two. Have a proper think, then click each one to reveal the answer — no peeking until you have tried.

Classic riddles

A good riddle is a small trapdoor in the language — obvious once you have fallen through it. Read each one, have a think, then click to drop the answer into view. No peeking until you have given it a real try.

I am full of keys but cannot open a single lock. What am I?

A piano — its keys make music, not entry.

The more you take away from me, the bigger I become. What am I?

A hole. Remove more and it only grows.

I have hands but cannot clap, and a face but never smile. What am I?

A clock.

What has to be broken before you can use it?

An egg.

I go up but never come down. What am I?

Your age.

What has a neck but no head, two arms but no hands?

A shirt (or sweater).

The more of this there is, the less you see. What is it?

Darkness.

What has many teeth but cannot bite?

A comb.

Logic puzzles

These reward careful reading rather than cleverness. Take them slowly, and perhaps keep a pencil handy to jot a note as you reason it out.

A farmer has 17 sheep, and all but 9 run away. How many are left?

Nine. “All but 9 run away” means nine remain.

A clock takes six seconds to strike seven o'clock (counting the gaps between the chimes). How long does it take to strike noon, twelve o'clock?

Eleven seconds. Seven strikes have six gaps, so each gap is one second; twelve strikes have eleven gaps.

Two mothers and two daughters sit down to lunch, yet there are only three plates and everyone has one. How?

They are a grandmother, her daughter, and her granddaughter — three people who are, between them, two mothers and two daughters.

A man looks at a photograph and says, “Brothers and sisters I have none, but that man's father is my father's son.” Who is in the photograph?

His son. “My father's son” is himself, so the man's father is him — the photo shows his son.

You have two coins that add up to 30 cents, and one of them is not a nickel. What are the two coins?

A quarter and a nickel. Only one of them is not a nickel — the quarter — and the condition says nothing about the other.

If it takes five minutes to boil one egg in a pan, how long to boil three eggs together in the same pan?

Five minutes — they boil at the same time, not one after another.

A rooster lays an egg on the exact peak of a barn roof. Which side does it roll down?

Neither — roosters do not lay eggs.

Number puzzles

A gentle pair for those who like figures. No advanced arithmetic required — just a clear head.

Using the numbers 1 through 9 once each, can you make three 3-digit numbers where the second is twice the first and the third is three times the first? (One well-known answer exists.)

Yes: 192, 384, and 576. Each digit from 1 to 9 is used exactly once, and 384 = 192 × 2, 576 = 192 × 3.

A snail climbs a 10-foot wall, rising 3 feet each day but slipping back 2 feet each night. On which day does it reach the top?

The eighth day. After seven days it has gained 7 feet; on the eighth day it climbs 3 more to reach 10 and is over the top before it can slip.

Why teasers are good company

A riddle or a logic puzzle works a little differently from a crossword or a quiz. Instead of reaching into your memory, it asks you to turn an idea over and look at it from another side. The pleasure is in the moment the trapdoor opens — the small, delighted “oh!” when the trick reveals itself. That moment is available to anyone, at any age, with nothing more than a quiet minute and a willingness to be fooled for a while.

Share these at the table, save them for a long car ride, or keep them for the grandchildren, who are often quicker than we expect and love nothing more than catching a grown-up out.

A few more to share

Here is another handful for the collection — the kind worth memorizing so you always have one ready when a grandchild says, “tell me a riddle.”

What gets wetter the more it dries?

A towel.

What has a thumb and four fingers but is not alive?

A glove.

What can travel all around the world while staying in one corner?

A postage stamp.

What has words but never speaks?

A book.

I am tall when I am young and short when I am old. What am I?

A candle.

What has a bottom at the top?

Your legs.

What runs around a whole yard without ever moving?

A fence.

What belongs to you, but other people use it far more than you do?

Your name.

What has a head and a tail but no body?

A coin.

What kind of band never plays any music?

A rubber band.

What comes down but never goes up?

Rain.

What has a ring but no finger?

A telephone — or a doorbell.

What building has the most stories?

A library.

What has four legs in the morning, two at midday, and three in the evening?

A person — crawling as a baby, walking as an adult, and using a cane in old age. This is the ancient riddle of the Sphinx.

What invention lets you look right through a wall?

A window.

What is so fragile that saying its name breaks it?

Silence.

What has many rings but no fingers?

A tree — count the rings to tell its age.

What two things can you never eat for breakfast?

Lunch and dinner.

What gets sharper the more you use it?

Your brain.

I have branches but no leaves, no trunk, and no fruit. What am I?

A bank — it has branches too.

What flies without wings?

Time.

What has a face and two hands but no arms or legs?

A clock, once again — the old favorites are worth repeating.

What can you catch but never throw?

A cold.

The ancient art of the riddle

Riddles are among the oldest word games we have. They appear in ancient Greek legend, in the Old English manuscripts of a thousand years ago, and in the folk tales of nearly every culture on earth. For most of human history, long before books were common, a clever riddle was both entertainment and a test of wit, passed by voice from one generation to the next around a fire or a table.

What has kept the riddle alive for so many centuries is the particular pleasure of its solution — that small, bright moment when the trick reveals itself and the impossible-seeming clue suddenly makes perfect sense. It is a pleasure that costs nothing, needs no equipment, and works just as well on a child as on a grandparent. When you share a riddle, you are taking part in one of the oldest and most democratic of all human games.

Teasers to share with the grandchildren

Few things delight a child more than being asked a riddle — and few things delight a grandparent more than watching a young face work one out. Brain teasers are a wonderful bridge between the generations, because they level the ground completely. A clever eight-year-old will often crack a riddle faster than any adult at the table, and the pride on their face is worth a hundred store-bought gifts.

Keep a few favorites in your back pocket for exactly these moments — a long car ride, a wait at a restaurant, a quiet afternoon when the young ones grow restless. The riddles on this page are chosen to be shareable: short enough to remember, old enough to be classics, and gentle enough that the answer brings a laugh rather than a groan. Read one aloud, let them stew on it, and resist the urge to give it away too soon; the struggle is the fun, and the triumph is theirs to earn.

How to crack a logic puzzle

Logic puzzles reward a different habit of mind from riddles. Where a riddle turns on a single hidden trick, a logic puzzle rewards patience and careful reading. The commonest mistake is to rush — to grab the first plausible answer before truly weighing the words. Slow down, and read the puzzle twice.

The real skill is to notice the exact wording, because a logic puzzle almost always hides its answer in a phrase we skim past. “All but nine run away” is not about the ones that leave; it is quietly telling you how many stay. “One of them is not a nickel” carefully says nothing about the other coin. When a puzzle seems impossible, the trick is nearly always a word you read too quickly, so go back and read it as if every word were chosen on purpose — because in a good puzzle, it was.
